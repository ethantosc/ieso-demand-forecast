# Data quality — Ontario hourly demand

**Status:** Milestone 1 complete · validated 2026-09-14

Machine-produced figures live in `data_quality_auto.md`, generated by
`src/ingest/check_demand.py`. Section numbers below refer to that file. Nothing
here restates a number from memory.

## 1. What is in the dataset

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | IESO public annual demand CSVs, `PUB_Demand_{year}.csv` |
| Coverage | 2002-05-01 to present (§1) |
| Rows | 213,624 hourly observations |
| Grain | one row per hour |
| Target variable | Market Demand (MW) — see spec §1 for why not Ontario Demand |

The series starts at market opening, 2002-05-01, because Market Demand is a market
construct that did not exist before that date. See §4 below.

**The current-year file is provisional.** `PUB_Demand_2026.csv` covers
2026-01-01 to 2026-09-13 and is still being written. Its last day is partial (one
hour). Any result computed from 2026 data is subject to change and must not be
treated as settled. `data/raw/demand/manifest.json` records the download timestamp
and SHA-256 of every file, so a changed file is detectable rather than mysterious.

## 2. Time convention

The raw CSV gives `Date` plus `Hour` 1–24, hour-**ending**. Hour 1 covers
00:00–01:00.

The staging table relabels to hour-beginning and carries three time columns:

| Column | Meaning | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| `ts_utc` | the actual instant, naive UTC | keys, joins, lags, sorting |
| `ts_local` | Toronto wall clock, naive | reading, plotting |
| `local_hour` | 0–23 wall clock | calendar features |

`ts_local` is **not unique** — the November fall-back hour appears twice each year,
24 occurrences across the series (§8). Never key on it.

## 3. The fixed-EST assumption and how it was tested

**Claim:** the CSV timeline is fixed EST (UTC−5 year round), not local wall clock.

**Why it matters:** it decides whether the series is continuous. If the files were
wall clock, one day a year would be missing an hour and another would have a
duplicate, and every 24h/168h lag crossing those days would be silently wrong.

**Correction to an earlier draft of this file.** Sections 7 and 8 were described as
the positive proof. They are not. Both are computed from `ts_utc`, which is itself
derived from the EST assumption, so they would show the same 23-hour and 25-hour
local dates whatever the raw file actually used. They are circular and cannot
confirm the claim. What they *do* confirm is that timezone handling matches the
historical rule change: transitions fall in April/October through 2006 and in
March/November from 2007 onward (§7), which is correct for North America.

The two pieces of evidence that are not circular:

**(a) Raw row counts (§2).** Every `delivery_date` carries exactly 24 rows, DST
transition days included, with no duplicate `(date, hour)` pairs (§4). A wall-clock
series cannot do this. The two exceptions are explained in §5 below and neither is
a transition day.

**(b) The August 2003 blackout (§11).** The Northeast blackout began at 16:11 EDT
on 2003-08-14. In this dataset the first fully collapsed hour is `ts_local`
2003-08-14 17:00 at 2,270 MW, against a normal August afternoon above 20,000 MW,
recovering through 2,366 / 2,673 / 3,480 / 4,162 / 4,737 MW over the following
hours. The 16:00–17:00 hour was only partially affected and stays above the
flagging threshold, exactly as a 16:11 start implies. Had the raw file been local
time and our conversion added a spurious hour, the collapse would appear at 18:00.
This is an external clock check against a documented event.

**Status: confirmed.** Fixed EST, for the whole 2002–2026 span.

**Still worth doing (Milestone 2).** A directly falsifiable test: plot the morning
load ramp in local time for the three days either side of a DST transition. The
morning ramp is alarm-clock driven, so it must begin at the same local hour before
and after the transition. A one-hour jump would falsify the convention.

## 4. Series start date

Market Demand exists only from market opening. The 2002 file begins 2002-05-01
(§1), which is that date.

Consequently the 1994-01-01 – 2002-04-30 file described in spec §2.1 is **out of
scope for v1**. It carries a pre-market Ontario demand derived from operational
meters; the v1 target variable does not exist within it. The "2002 seam" question
in the spec appendix does not get answered, it dissolves.

The seam that does exist — between consecutive annual files — is addressed in §6.

## 5. Missing and implausible values

**One real gap.** `PUB_Demand_2025.csv` holds 8,759 rows instead of 8,760 (§1).
The missing observation is 2025-05-01 hour-ending 1, i.e. 00:00–01:00 EST
(§2), and it shows up as a two-hour step in the UTC series at 2025-05-01 05:00 UTC
(§5). This is a gap in IESO's published file, not a parsing artifact.

**Nothing has been imputed or dropped.** The gap is left as a gap. Any model or
backtest must handle it explicitly; a lag that reaches across it should return a
null rather than a wrong number.

**Two apparent failures that are not defects:**

- 2002-05-01 shows 23 local hours (§7). This is the start of the series:
  00:00 EST is 01:00 EDT, so the first local date begins at 01:00.
- 2026-09-13 shows 1 row in §2 and 2 rows in §7. This is the current partial day
  at the end of the series. Exclude the final incomplete day when modelling.

**No nulls anywhere** (§10).

**Values outside 5,000–30,000 MW** (§11) are the seven blackout hours discussed in
§3. They are genuine observations of a real event, not errors, and must not be
cleaned. They will, however, distort any model trained across 2003-08-14; treat
that day as a known outlier at modelling time rather than deleting it here.

## 6. Year-file seams

Twenty-four boundaries between consecutive annual files (§6). Deltas across the
31 Dec 23:00 → 1 Jan 00:00 EST boundary range from −1,118 MW to +249 MW, which is
the same order as an ordinary overnight hour-to-hour change.

**No level shift at any seam.** The annual files agree on definition and units.
The 2002 → 2003 boundary is −883 MW, unremarkable among the rest.

One item to eyeball during EDA: the 2024 → 2025 boundary has a delta of exactly
0.0 MW, with 17,247 MW on both sides. Most likely coincidence, but an exact repeat
sitting precisely on a file boundary is worth a look.

## 7. Market vs Ontario demand

Market Demand = Ontario Demand + scheduled exports, so the gap should be positive
and material. It is, and it grows: from an average of 306 MW in 2002 to
2,000–2,500 MW in recent years (§12). This is the same relationship that decided
the target variable, holding in the actuals and not only in the DATotals forecasts.

**43 hours run the other way** — Ontario exceeding Market demand, spread over 2004,
2009, 2024, 2025 and 2026, the largest at −817 MW (§13). That is 0.02% of the
series, and the magnitudes are small relative to demand. Flagged, not corrected,
cause unestablished.

## 8. Findings that affect modelling

Two things in §14 change how the project should be built, so they are recorded here
rather than left for the EDA notebook.

**Ontario is not purely summer-peaking.** Six of twenty-five annual peaks fall in
winter: 2004 (26 Jan), 2014 (7 Jan), 2015 (7 Jan), 2017 (31 Dec), 2018 (3 Jan) and
2023 (3 Feb). Spec §2.2 overstates the summer-peaking characterisation. Practical
consequences: HDD is not a secondary feature, and the seasonal error decomposition
required by spec §5 is load-bearing rather than decorative. Summer peaks land at
14:00–17:00 local and winter peaks at 17:00–18:00 local, which is consistent with
air-conditioning versus evening lighting and heating.

**There is a trend break, and it has recently reversed.** Annual mean Market
Demand ran 18,500–19,450 MW over 2005–2008, fell to a trough of 17,189 MW in 2017,
and has risen since: 18,022 MW in 2024, 18,896 MW in 2025, 19,651 MW in the partial
2026. The 2026 figure is not comparable (partial year) but 2024 → 2025 is. The
2026-07-02 peak of 27,678 MW is the highest since 2011.

This is why spec §5's instruction to run both a 3-year rolling window and a
full-history expanding window matters: a model fitted across 2005–2015 is
learning a demand level that no longer applies.

## 9. Known limitations

1. **DST.** The series is fixed EST; local wall-clock dates therefore have one
   23-hour and one 25-hour day per year, and `ts_local` is not unique. Any analysis
   keyed on local time will double-count the November fall-back hour.
2. **One missing hour**, 2025-05-01 00:00 EST, left unimputed.
3. **Weather forecast gap.** Track A vs track B, see spec §4. Not yet started.
4. **Station representativeness.** Pending the ECCC decision, spec §2.2.
5. **Definitional stability unverified.** Whether IESO's definition of Market
   Demand changed at any point over twenty-four years has not been checked. The
   absence of level shifts at file seams (§6) is weak evidence against a sudden
   change but says nothing about a gradual one.
6. **Current-year data is provisional** and will change as 2026 fills in.
